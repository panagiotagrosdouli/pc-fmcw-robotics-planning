\title{From QoS Prediction to Decision Validity:\\
Measurement-Supported Counterfactual Replay for Communication-Aware Vehicle Planning}

\author{Panagiota Grosdouli%

\begin{abstract}
Predictive quality-of-service (QoS) models and communication-aware motion planners are established research directions, but logged field measurements create a decision-validity problem: a planner evaluates actions that were not actually taken, while the dataset contains outcomes only along the driven trajectory. We study this mismatch using the CICV5G field-measurement dataset and a measurement-supported counterfactual replay protocol. Complete acquisition runs are separated across training, calibration, and test partitions to reduce route and temporal leakage. Causal QoS predictors are benchmarked against persistence at multiple planning horizons, while candidate queries are audited for empirical support using training-measurement proximity and density. For decision evaluation, candidate actions are restricted to future states recorded later in the same held-out run; their measured future delay is hidden during scoring and revealed only after selection. One-step delay prediction is dominated by persistence, whereas calibration-gated spatial/context information becomes useful at longer planning horizons. Predictive planning has a favorable measured-delay direction relative to a reactive baseline across all five grouped split assignments, although the primary paired comparison does not remain significant after the declared Holm correction. A support-aware planner consistently reduces unsupported selections but does not provide a stable additional QoS gain. The contribution is a reproducible decision-validity framework that separates predictive utility, empirical measurement support, and measured post-selection evaluability when field-measured communication data are used to influence vehicle motion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Connected and automated vehicles increasingly depend on wireless communication for cooperative perception, cloud-assisted services, infrastructure interaction, and distributed decision making. Motion and communication are therefore coupled: vehicle position changes the link conditions it experiences, while anticipated link conditions can influence the motion selected by a planner. This coupling is not new. Communication-aware motion planning has a long history \cite{ghaffarkhah2011,muralidharan2021}, including obstacle-aware planning \cite{hurst2021}, joint motion--communication--sensing optimization \cite{cai2022}, and more recent radio-map-based planning \cite{gordon2026}. QoS-aware autonomous-vehicle trajectory selection has also been studied directly \cite{ullah2025}.

In parallel, predictive QoS (PQoS) has become an established topic in vehicular communications. Field campaigns and public datasets support data-driven prediction of throughput, delay, and radio indicators \cite{sliwa2018,hernangomez2023,palaios2023,partani2025,zhang2026}. Recent work also predicts dynamic radio maps \cite{cheng2026} and integrates radio-prediction uncertainty into motion control \cite{kim2026}. Consequently, a paper whose sole claim is \emph{predict connectivity and add it to a planner} would overlap substantially with prior work.

The present study addresses a different question: \emph{what evidence is required before a prediction learned from logged field measurements should be allowed to justify a different vehicle motion decision?} This question appears when predictive communication models are evaluated beyond pure regression. A supervised predictor is trained on states that were observed. A planner, however, asks counterfactual questions about candidate future states. If a candidate was never visited, the logged dataset generally contains no measured communication outcome for that action. A planner can therefore appear successful if evaluation silently assigns model predictions, interpolated maps, or future information as if they were measured truth.

This distinction is especially important for spatially and temporally correlated vehicular data. Random sample-level splits can leak neighboring measurements from the same drive into both training and test sets. Palaios et al. show that split choice can substantially alter apparent QoS-prediction performance \cite{palaios2023}. Standard split-conformal guarantees also rely on exchangeability and can degrade under distribution shift \cite{zou2024}. Thus, prediction accuracy, uncertainty calibration, empirical support, and decision evaluation should not be collapsed into a single notion of trust.

We organize the paper around three validity questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Predictive validity:} does future QoS contain incremental information beyond a strong causal baseline at the horizon where a planner can act?
\item \textbf{Empirical-support validity:} is a candidate-state prediction made in a region represented by the measurements used to train the model?
\item \textbf{Outcome validity:} can the consequence of the selected counterfactual action be evaluated using an actual withheld measurement rather than a fabricated radio-map value?
\end{enumerate}

To study these questions, we use CICV5G \cite{zhang2026}, a field-measured vehicular 5G/V2N2V dataset. The archived repository pipeline uses 38 complete acquisition runs comprising 43,045 synchronized samples. Runs, rather than rows, are partitioned into training, calibration, and test sets. Future-delay prediction is compared against persistence over multiple planning horizons. Empirical support is computed only from training measurements. Finally, the planner is evaluated using \emph{measurement-supported counterfactual replay} (MSCR): candidate choices are restricted to future states that are actually present later in the held-out run, while their measured future delay remains hidden until after the planner selects an action.

The contributions are deliberately scoped:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formulate a \emph{decision-validity decomposition} for logged communication data that separates predictive accuracy, empirical measurement support, and post-decision evaluability.
\item We implement a whole-run leakage-resistant PQoS benchmark with persistence as the mandatory causal baseline and explicitly evaluate the horizon at which learned spatial/context information adds value.
\item We implement an empirical support audit for trajectory-conditioned QoS queries using only training measurements, rather than interpreting predictive uncertainty alone as evidence of in-distribution support.
\item We formulate MSCR, an offline replay protocol in which every evaluated candidate has a subsequently measured outcome, but that outcome is causally hidden until after action selection.
\item We use P1--P3 comparisons to separate predictive decision utility from support-risk control and retain null/negative results when the support-aware planner does not further improve QoS.
\end{enumerate}

These contributions are methodological and empirical. We do not claim novelty for communication-aware planning, radio maps, PQoS, uncertainty-aware MPC, or counterfactual world models in general.

\section{Related Work and Non-Overlap Positioning}
\subsection{Communication-aware motion planning}
Ghaffarkhah and Mostofi introduced a probabilistic framework in which wireless-channel structure is learned from measurements and incorporated into robot motion planning \cite{ghaffarkhah2011}. The broader field now includes theoretical and practical formulations for exploiting robot motion to preserve or improve communication \cite{muralidharan2021}, obstacle-aware communication planning \cite{hurst2021}, and joint motion--communication--sensing optimization in real wireless channel environments \cite{cai2022}. Gordon et al. estimate radio maps online and convert them into service-specific risk maps used by a motion planner \cite{gordon2026}. Kim et al. combine Gaussian-process RSSI prediction with adaptive tube MPC and explicitly propagate radio-map and motion uncertainty \cite{kim2026}. These works establish that communication-aware motion and uncertainty-aware radio-map planning are existing ideas.

The present paper therefore does not propose a new motion-planning family. Its planner is intentionally simple so that the study can isolate whether logged communication evidence is valid for evaluating predictive decisions.

\subsection{Predictive QoS from vehicular measurements}
Sliwa et al. combined mobility prediction and learned connectivity maps for context-predictive car-to-cloud communication and evaluated the approach in a field campaign \cite{sliwa2018}. The Berlin V2X dataset later provided high-resolution, GPS-located measurements from multiple vehicles and radio access technologies \cite{hernangomez2023}. Palaios et al. studied the end-to-end ML workflow for vehicular QoS prediction and highlighted the sensitivity of results to dataset splitting \cite{palaios2023}. Partani et al. further improved urban V2X throughput prediction using information from leading vehicles and historical trends \cite{partani2025}. These studies show that field-measured PQoS and data-split methodology are established topics.

Our distinction is that predictor performance is not the endpoint. The predictor is inserted into a motion-decision experiment in which the validity of each candidate query and the availability of a measured post-decision outcome are audited explicitly.

\subsection{Predictive radio maps and task-aware world models}
RadioMapMotion targets proactive spatio-temporal radio-environment prediction and provides a benchmark for forecasting future radio maps \cite{cheng2026}. RMWorld goes further by valuing channel information according to downstream task relevance and by filtering counterfactual radio-world-model rollouts using credibility criteria \cite{wang2026rmworld}. These developments make it inappropriate to claim that future radio prediction, counterfactual communication rollouts, or task-aware communication modeling are themselves novel.

The difference in this work is the evidence source and evaluation boundary. We do not treat a learned world model as counterfactual ground truth. Instead, we restrict offline decision evaluation to candidate states that have an actual future measurement in the logged held-out trajectory.

\subsection{Vehicular QoS-aware trajectory planning}
Ullah et al. optimize autonomous-vehicle routes using network spectral-efficiency information in a simulated 6G/mmWave coverage setting \cite{ullah2025}. This establishes QoS-aware vehicle trajectory optimization as prior art. The present study asks a narrower question: when the QoS surface comes from finite field measurements rather than a simulator or complete coverage map, which counterfactual decisions are empirically supportable and which can be evaluated without inventing unmeasured outcomes?

\section{Data and Leakage-Controlled Experimental Design}
\subsection{CICV5G field measurements}
CICV5G provides field-measured 5G V2N2V delay with synchronized network and vehicle context, including variables such as RSRP, SINR, cell identity, position, heading, velocity, and timestamps \cite{zhang2026}. The source publication reports more than 150,000 synchronized records across multiple scenarios; the present archived study uses a defined 38-run W2S subset containing 43,045 samples.

The primary grouped split contains 22 training runs (22,441 samples), 7 calibration runs (8,846 samples), and 9 test runs (11,758 samples). These data support only the measured 5G/V2N2V branch. They are not evidence for the separate PC-FMCW optical model in the repository.

\subsection{Whole-run partitioning}
Let the complete set of acquisition runs be
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{tr}}\dot\cup
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cal}}\dot\cup
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{te}}.
\end{equation}
Every sample from one run belongs to exactly one partition. Predictors and empirical support are fitted using $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{tr}}$. Hyperparameter/fusion selection and residual uncertainty calibration use $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cal}}$. Final planner evaluation uses $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{te}}$.

This design is intentionally stricter than a random row split. Adjacent vehicular measurements are highly correlated in time and space, and prior work has shown that random split choices can substantially overstate PQoS performance \cite{palaios2023}.

Five grouped split assignments are used for sensitivity analysis. Because the same finite collection of drives is reused, the five assignments are dependent robustness configurations, not five independent replications.

\section{Decision-Validity Decomposition}
We define decision validity as an audit rather than a scalar probability. A candidate can fail for different scientific reasons even if its predicted QoS appears favorable.

\subsection{Layer I: predictive validity at the decision horizon}
For run $r$, decision time $t$, and horizon $h$, let measured delay be
\begin{equation}
y^{(h)}_{r,t}=y_{r,t+h}.
\end{equation}
The causal persistence baseline is
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{\mathrm{pers}}_{r,t+h|t}=y_{r,t}.
\end{equation}
A learned spatial/context model produces
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{\mathrm{learn}}_{r,t+h|t}
=f_h(x^{\mathrm{cand}}_{r,t+h},c_{r,t}),
\end{equation}
where $c_{r,t}$ contains only information available at decision time.

A calibration-gated predictor combines the two:
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{\mathrm{fuse}}
=(1-\alpha_h)\hat y^{\mathrm{pers}}
+\alpha_h\hat y^{\mathrm{learn}},
\qquad \alpha_h\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_h$ chosen only from calibration runs. The learned predictor is therefore not assumed useful merely because it is more complex.

\subsection{Layer II: empirical measurement support}
Let
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{x_i:i\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}
\end{equation}
be the training-position set. For candidate position $x$, define
\begin{equation}
d_{\min}(x)=\min_{x_i\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\|x-x_i\|_2
\end{equation}
and local density
\begin{equation}
n_\rho(x)=\sum_{x_i\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{tr}}}
\mathbf{1}\{\|x-x_i\|_2\le\rho\}.
\end{equation}
A frozen empirical-support rule is
\begin{equation}
u(x)=\mathbf{1}\{d_{\min}(x)>d_{\max}\;\lor\;n_\rho(x)<n_{\min}\}.
\end{equation}
The thresholds are experimental support controls, not physical channel parameters.

This layer is kept distinct from predictive uncertainty. A predictor may return a narrow residual interval in a region poorly represented by training measurements. Conversely, a well-supported region may still have high intrinsic QoS variability.

Residual split-conformal intervals are built from calibration residuals. They are used only as empirical uncertainty diagnostics. Ordinary split-conformal coverage depends on exchangeability and should not be assumed to remain nominal under arbitrary grouped distribution shift \cite{zou2024}.

\subsection{Layer III: measured post-decision evaluability}
For offline evaluation, a counterfactual action should have a measurable outcome that was not exposed to the planner. We therefore require candidate actions to map to future samples actually recorded in the same held-out run. If no such measurement exists, the candidate is not used as evidence for measured decision benefit.

This criterion is an evaluation constraint, not a deployment restriction. In a deployed vehicle, the planner can consider unvisited states; the point is that a logged dataset cannot retrospectively certify the real QoS outcome at an arbitrary unvisited location.

\section{Measurement-Supported Counterfactual Replay}
For held-out run $r$ and decision time $t$, define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_{r,t}=
\{a^{(1)}_{r,t},\ldots,a^{(K)}_{r,t}\},
\end{equation}
where each candidate maps to a later recorded state $x_{r,t+h_k}$. The predictor may use the candidate state and causally available context, but the corresponding future measured delay $y_{r,t+h_k}$ is hidden during scoring.

After a planner chooses
\begin{equation}
a^\star_P\in\mathcal{A}_{r,t},
\end{equation}
the recorded outcome
\begin{equation}
y^{\mathrm{meas}}_{P,r,t}=y_{r,t+h^\star}
\end{equation}
is revealed solely for evaluation.

MSCR therefore enforces two boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{causal selection:} no planner can inspect the future measurement attached to a candidate;
\item \textbf{measured evaluation:} every reported post-selection QoS outcome comes from a field measurement, not from the predictor being evaluated.
\end{enumerate}

The protocol does not recover the full causal effect of physically driving an alternative trajectory, because choosing a different route could itself change future traffic, network load, and radio geometry. It is therefore described as route-constrained measured replay, not as a real-world intervention experiment.

\section{Planner Family}
Let $J_{\mathrm{mob}}(a)$ denote mobility deviation/cost and $\hat y(a)$ predicted future delay.

\paragraph{P0: Mobility/reference}
\begin{equation}
a^\star_{P0}=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}J_{\mathrm{mob}}(a).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{P1: Reactive/current-QoS}
\begin{equation}
a^\star_{P1}
=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}
\left[J_{\mathrm{mob}}(a)+\lambda_c\hat y^{\mathrm{pers}}(a)\right].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{P2: Predictive communication}
\begin{equation}
a^\star_{P2}
=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}
\left[J_{\mathrm{mob}}(a)+\lambda_c\hat y^{\mathrm{fuse}}(a)\right].
\end{equation}
P2 isolates the value of future QoS information relative to the reactive baseline.

\paragraph{P3: Predictive communication with support control}
\begin{equation}
a^\star_{P3}
=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}
\left[J_{\mathrm{mob}}(a)+\lambda_c\hat y^{\mathrm{fuse}}(a)
+\lambda_s u(a)\right].
\end{equation}
P3 tests whether the planner can reduce reliance on weakly supported predictions. It is not assumed to improve communication QoS over P2.

\section{Evaluation and Statistical Protocol}
Prediction metrics include delay MAE/RMSE, empirical interval coverage, and support-stratified error. Decision metrics include selected measured delay, the fraction of selected outcomes above an experimental 50-ms delay threshold, changed-decision fraction, unsupported-selection fraction, and mobility deviation.

Within one held-out split, planner comparisons are paired by acquisition run. The run, not the timestamp, is the inferential unit. Paired bootstrap intervals and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are reported. Holm correction is applied across the predeclared planner/metric family. Raw $p$-values that do not survive multiplicity correction are retained as exploratory diagnostics only.

The five grouped split assignments are summarized descriptively by effect direction, mean, and range. They are not converted into an artificial $n=5$ hypothesis test because runs recur across assignments.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Persistence is the short-horizon baseline to beat}
On the archived primary grouped split, one-step persistence achieves delay MAE 5.576 ms and RMSE 20.914 ms. Spatial kNN, Random Forest, and Extra Trees obtain 10.862, 7.150, and 6.659 ms MAE, respectively. The negative result is central: generic learned spatial/tree models do not automatically outperform the current QoS measurement.

This finding is consistent with the broader PQoS literature's emphasis on split methodology and strong baselines \cite{palaios2023}. It also motivates evaluating prediction at the horizon where motion can actually change.

\subsection{Learned information becomes useful at planning horizons}
Across five grouped split assignments, calibration-gated prediction improves delay MAE over persistence in all five assignments at 20, 50, and 100 steps. The corresponding mean improvements are approximately 1.460, 2.506, and 2.622 ms. The median physical horizons are approximately 1.1, 2.8, and 5.5 s.

At 1, 5, and 10 steps, effects are smaller and less consistent. We therefore do not claim universal superiority of learned prediction. The supported conclusion is horizon-dependent: spatial/context information has incremental value at the longer decision horizons tested here.

\subsection{Empirical support diagnoses a distinct failure mode}
On the primary split, delay MAE is 5.332 ms for queries within 1 m of a training measurement, 7.120 ms at 1--5 m, and 9.579 ms at 5--15 m. Empirical conformal coverage decreases from 0.888 to 0.864 and 0.761 across the same bins.

A deeper route/context audit shows that support distance is not a universal monotonic error law; the most severe sparse-support degradation is concentrated in particular contexts. Support is therefore used as a validity diagnostic rather than as a deterministic propagation model.

\subsection{Predictive planning changes measured outcomes}
Table~\ref{tab:replay} reports the primary held-out replay over nine acquisition runs. P1 obtains 23.318 ms mean measured delay and a 0.02179 fraction above the experimental 50-ms threshold. P2 reduces these to 22.526 ms and 0.01900 while changing 2.51\% of decisions and incurring 0.01254 mobility deviation.

\subsection{Support control trades mobility for evidential conservatism}
On the primary split, P3 reduces unsupported-selection exposure from 0.15634 under P2 to 0.13798. Mean measured delay changes only from 22.526 to 22.598 ms, while mobility deviation increases from 0.01254 to 0.02305.

Across five grouped split assignments, P3--P2 unsupported-selection effects are approximately
\[
[-0.0184,-0.0153,-0.0190,-0.0562,-0.0309],
\]
all favoring P3, with descriptive mean approximately $-0.0280$. In contrast, P3--P2 measured-delay effects have mixed sign and descriptive mean near zero. The data therefore support a validity--mobility trade-off, not a claim that empirical-support penalties automatically improve QoS.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Why this is not another radio-map-planning paper}
Prior work already demonstrates that radio maps, predicted QoS, and communication-risk models can improve motion planning \cite{ghaffarkhah2011,gordon2026,ullah2025,kim2026}. Repeating that claim with another planner would add limited novelty. The present result is instead about \emph{evidence discipline}: what can be concluded when communication estimates come from finite logged field data.

MSCR deliberately gives up arbitrary off-route planning freedom during evaluation in exchange for a stronger outcome statement: the communication value used to score the selected action after the fact is measured, not generated by the same model family being assessed. This prevents a circular evaluation pattern in which a learned or analytical radio map both drives the decision and defines the truth used to declare that decision successful.

\subsection{Prediction accuracy is not decision utility}
The one-step study would suggest that a learned predictor is unnecessary because persistence is stronger than the tested spatial/tree baselines. The longer-horizon study reaches a different conclusion. When the horizon approaches the time scale on which motion can change, calibration-gated spatial/context information adds predictive value. The relevant baseline is therefore not machine learning versus no machine learning but future prediction versus the strongest causal information available at the same decision time.

\subsection{Uncertainty is not empirical support}
A predictive interval and an empirical-support score answer different questions. The former summarizes observed residual behavior under the calibration procedure. The latter asks whether the candidate resembles states represented in the training data. Under distribution shift, nominal conformal coverage may degrade \cite{zou2024}. Treating a narrow interval as proof that an extrapolative query is trustworthy would therefore be unjustified.

P3 operationalizes this distinction. It reduces unsupported selections consistently, yet does not improve QoS consistently. That negative result is informative: evidential conservatism has a mobility cost and should not be marketed as a free performance gain.

\subsection{Relationship to task-aware counterfactual models}
Recent task-aware radio world models explicitly reason about decision-relevant model error and counterfactual rollout credibility \cite{wang2026rmworld}. Our setting is complementary rather than competitive. RMWorld addresses how a model should learn and select informative rollouts for control. We address how logged field measurements can be used to \emph{evaluate} motion decisions without silently converting model-generated counterfactuals into measured outcomes.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
First, MSCR is offline route-constrained replay, not a randomized intervention or closed-loop vehicle experiment. Selecting a different physical trajectory could alter future radio geometry, traffic interactions, handovers, and network load. The replay therefore measures decision value only within the logged route-supported candidate construction.

Second, the current support model is intentionally simple. Euclidean distance and local training density cannot capture all relevant distribution shift, such as network-cell changes, temporal load, blockage, or context interactions. The support audit should be interpreted as an explicit lower-level diagnostic, not as a complete OOD detector.

Third, the residual conformal intervals are not assumed to retain exact nominal coverage under grouped distribution shift. Their observed coverage is reported empirically and the limitations of exchangeability are acknowledged \cite{zou2024}.

Fourth, the primary held-out set contains nine runs. Although the paired bootstrap interval for P2--P1 mean delay excludes zero, the corresponding Wilcoxon result does not remain significant after the declared Holm family correction. The result is therefore reported as effect-size and directional robustness evidence, not confirmatory superiority.

Fifth, the repeated split assignments reuse the same 38 drives and are dependent. Directional consistency across them is a sensitivity result rather than independent replication.

Finally, the CICV5G measurements validate only the measured 5G/V2N2V decision study. They do not validate the separate PC-FMCW optical channel model maintained elsewhere in the repository.

\section{Reproducibility and Data Availability}
The CICV5G dataset is documented by Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2026} and archived under Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17475688. The repository records the exact acquisition subset, grouped split manifests, predictor configurations, empirical-support rules, replay implementation, and statistical-analysis scripts.

The publication workflow separates source measurements from generated artifacts and records the grouping unit used for inference. Numerical paper assets are regenerated from archived CSV outputs rather than manually transcribed. Hosted-run timing is retained only as an implementation diagnostic because shared-runner load produces material wall-clock variation; no embedded real-time guarantee is claimed.

\section{Conclusion}
Predictive QoS and communication-aware planning are mature research areas; the unresolved methodological issue studied here is how logged field measurements can support counterfactual motion decisions without overstating what was actually observed. By separating predictive validity, empirical support, and measured outcome evaluability, the proposed study reframes connectivity planning as an evidence-validity problem.

On CICV5G, persistence is a strong one-step baseline, while calibration-gated spatial/context information becomes useful at longer planning horizons. In measurement-supported counterfactual replay, predictive P2 reduces measured delay relative to reactive P1 in direction across all five grouped split assignments. Support-aware P3 consistently reduces unsupported selections, but does not provide a stable additional QoS benefit and incurs additional mobility deviation.

The resulting conclusion is intentionally narrow: field-measured connectivity prediction should influence motion only after its causal horizon, empirical support, and evaluation boundary have been made explicit. This provides a reproducible path from PQoS regression to defensible decision-level evidence without treating unobserved counterfactual states as if they had measured ground truth.
