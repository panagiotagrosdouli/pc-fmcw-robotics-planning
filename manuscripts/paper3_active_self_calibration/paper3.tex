\title{Decision-Triggered Active Self-Calibration for PC-FMCW-Informed Vehicular Optical Planning}
\author{Panagiota Grosdouli}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Uncertainty in a communication model can change which trajectory a connected vehicle should choose. Deliberately moving to learn that model, however, is useful only when the additional information can affect the decision. This paper studies a decision-triggered active self-calibration strategy for a modeled directional optical link embedded in receding-horizon vehicular planning. Five planners share the same candidate generation, target prediction, and hard safety filters, and differ only in how they use link-model uncertainty. A 27-setting development sweep on 20 seeds selected one setting before 50 untouched confirmatory seeds were evaluated across six scenarios. In confirmation, unconditional information seeking increased cumulative decision regret by 0.083290 relative to passive Bayesian calibration. Decision triggering recovered 0.082211 of that penalty and reduced probe fraction by 0.037444, with both effects surviving Holm correction. The benefit did not extend to superiority over passive calibration: C3 had higher descriptive regret than C1 overall, and in the decision-critical scenario it probed without reducing regret. A distance-only ablation suppressed nontrivial probing, while a measured vehicular visible-light study showed essentially no directional predictive gain. The results support decision relevance as a practical guard against unnecessary probing, while leaving the value of active calibration beyond passive Bayesian updating unresolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Communication-aware motion planning has moved from a networking concern to a planning concern. Mobile robots have long been routed around poor-connectivity regions \cite{ghaffarkhah2011}; more recent work incorporates online radio maps directly into navigation \cite{gordon2026}, while autonomous-vehicle planners have been designed around communication quality-of-service targets \cite{ullah2025}. Vehicular ISAC pushes the coupling further by treating sensing, communication, and motion as parts of the same decision process \cite{cheng2022,jin2026}.

A second layer of difficulty appears when the communication model itself is uncertain. In that case, vehicle motion does more than change geometry: it also changes what can be learned about the link. This is the familiar dual-control tension between acting for the task at hand and acting to improve future decisions \cite{barshalom1974,mesbah2018}. In a vehicle, that tradeoff is especially sharp. A maneuver can be informative and still be a poor motion decision.

The key idea in this paper is that parameter uncertainty alone is not enough to justify probing. A posterior may remain broad while every plausible model selects the same safe trajectory. Conversely, a relatively compact posterior can matter if its remaining hypotheses disagree about the best action. We therefore trigger active calibration from \emph{decision relevance}: whether model uncertainty changes the ranking of the available safe trajectories and whether choosing the belief-optimal trajectory carries expected regret under plausible parameter values.

We study this idea in a controlled PC-FMCW-informed analytical simulation. The latent optical quantities are simulator parameters rather than measured calibration constants. Five planners, C0--C4, share the same candidate lattice, causal target prediction, and hard feasibility filters; they differ only in how they represent and use uncertainty in the modeled link. Development and confirmation use disjoint seed ranges, and the confirmatory analysis is performed at the independent seed level.

The contribution is threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We introduce a decision-triggered active-calibration planner that enables information seeking only when posterior model uncertainty changes the downstream trajectory decision. The C0--C4 family isolates nominal planning, passive Bayesian calibration, unconditional probing, decision-triggered probing, and a model-relative oracle within one common safety interface.
\item We evaluate the mechanism under a frozen development/confirmatory protocol with paired bootstrap intervals, paired Wilcoxon tests, effect sizes, and Holm correction. The central empirical result is asymmetric: C3 removes most of the large penalty introduced by unconditional C2, but it does not establish an advantage over passive C1.
\item We examine why through two mechanism checks: a distance-only ablation that removes directional dependence and separately scoped measured-data studies for vehicular visible light and 5G V2X. These studies are used to bound the interpretation of the simulator results rather than to retrofit physical validation.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting question is deliberately specific: can decision relevance tell a planner when \emph{not} to spend motion on calibration, and under what conditions does the remaining active probe actually help?
\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Dual Control and Active Uncertainty Reduction}
The dual effect formalizes the fact that a control action may influence both the physical state and the information available for future decisions \cite{barshalom1974}. Exact dual control is generally difficult, and modern stochastic-MPC work often relies on tractable approximations that retain some exploration--exploitation structure \cite{mesbah2018,klenske2016}. Online experiment-design MPC has similarly introduced deliberate excitation during normal control operation, with the important observation that excitation should disappear once it no longer has value \cite{heirung2015}.

Recent robotics work brings the same idea into motion planning. Active-learning dual control has been used for auto-optimization \cite{li2025}; shielding-aware dual control has been developed for safe interactive driving \cite{hu2024}; and active estimation has been coupled with path planning in robotic assembly \cite{pashupathy2026}. Our method belongs to this approximate dual-control family, but uses a simpler question to decide when exploration is warranted: do plausible link models actually disagree about the motion decision?

\subsection{Informative Motion and Calibration}
Informative path planning chooses trajectories partly for the measurements they generate \cite{hitz2017,popovic2020}. Calibration methods use a related idea, exploiting motion geometry to expose observable directions in the parameter space. Examples include observability-aware LiDAR--IMU calibration \cite{lv2022} and optimal-experiment-design trajectories for robotic camera calibration \cite{amersdorfer2025}.

The distinction here is the endpoint. We do not reward parameter accuracy for its own sake. Information is useful only if it improves the downstream trajectory decision, which is why the main metric is decision regret rather than calibration error alone.

\subsection{Communication-Aware Planning and Vehicular Optical Links}
Communication-aware planning is established prior art \cite{ghaffarkhah2011}. Recent examples include navigation with online radio-map estimates \cite{gordon2026}, autonomous-vehicle trajectory planning around communication QoS \cite{ullah2025}, planning-oriented ISAC \cite{cheng2022,jin2026}, and optical-communication-aware NMPC for aerial robots \cite{silano2025}. Our setting adds uncertainty in the communication model itself, so motion can influence both link quality and the belief used to evaluate future motion.

Vehicular optical communication provides the physical motivation for a directional link model. Outdoor optical V2V systems have been demonstrated with LED transmitters and camera receivers \cite{takai2014}; vehicular VLC models account for distance, lateral geometry, transmitter characteristics, weather, and receiver properties \cite{karbalayghareh2020}; and measurement-driven studies have examined path loss, non-line-of-sight propagation, vertical vehicle motion, and adaptive receiver field of view \cite{turan2021,turan2022nlos,mohamed2023,cailean2024}. These studies motivate the role of geometry, but they do not identify the simulator parameters used here. The latent vector below should therefore be read as a controlled model-mismatch family, not as a measured optical calibration.
\section{System Model}
\subsection{Receding-Horizon Planning and Common Safety Set}
At planning step $k$, the ego vehicle state is
\begin{equation}
x_k=[p_{x,k},p_{y,k},\psi_k,v_k]^\top .
\end{equation}
A causal predictor maps the observed target history to a common future target sequence $\hat q_{k+j|k}$. Every planner generates the same bounded candidate lattice from lateral offsets $\{-1,0,1\}$~m, candidate horizons $\{2,3\}$~s, and speed offsets $\{-1.5,0,1.5\}$~m/s.

Let $\mathcal{T}_k$ denote the generated candidate set. Before any communication or information score is evaluated, candidates pass identical road, speed, static-obstacle, time-aligned dynamic-target, and terminal stop-viability checks. The resulting set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{safe}}_k
=
\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_k:\tau \text{ satisfies all hard feasibility tests}\}.
\end{equation}
The planning boundary is therefore strict: an informative trajectory cannot become feasible because of its information value. The implementation first uses a 0.5-m planning buffer beyond the 2.0-m physical collision distance and permits the same hierarchical fallback to the physical boundary for every planner if the buffered set is empty.

\subsection{Modeled Directional Optical Link}
The uncertain simulator parameter is
\begin{equation}
\phi=[\alpha_{\mathrm{loss}},\delta_{\mathrm{beam}},k_{\mathrm{angular}}]^\top ,
\end{equation}
with nominal value $\phi_0=[1,0,1]^\top$. Here $\alpha_{\mathrm{loss}}>0$ scales the distance-loss exponent, $\delta_{\mathrm{beam}}$ is a modeled boresight offset, and $k_{\mathrm{angular}}>0$ scales angular attenuation.

For relative distance $d$ and wrapped angular error
\begin{equation}
e_\theta=\operatorname{wrap}\!\left(
\operatorname{atan2}(\Delta y,\Delta x)-\psi-\delta_{\mathrm{beam}}
\right),
\end{equation}
the implemented distance and angular terms are
\begin{align}
L_d(\phi)
&=10n_0\alpha_{\mathrm{loss}}
\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\max(d,0.1)}{d_0}\right),\\
g_\theta(\phi)
&=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{angular}}
\left(\frac{e_\theta}{\sigma_\theta}\right)^2\right],\\
L_\theta(\phi)
&=-10\log_{10}\!\left(\max(g_\theta,\epsilon)\right).
\end{align}
The modeled SNR is
\begin{equation}
\gamma(\phi)=\gamma_0-L_d(\phi)-L_\theta(\phi).
\end{equation}

The model maps SNR through a soft outage threshold to modeled outage probability, BER, and goodput. For candidate $\tau$, the connectivity component used by the planner is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
C(\tau,\phi)
=
0.7\,\overline{p}_{\mathrm{out}}
+
0.3\left[1-\prod_j(1-p_{\mathrm{out},j})\right].
\end{equation}
The candidate objective under parameter hypothesis $\phi$ is
\begin{equation}
J(\tau,\phi)=M(\tau)+\lambda_c C(\tau,\phi),
\end{equation}
where $M(\tau)$ is the common mobility cost and $\lambda_c=1$ in the frozen study.

This model is \emph{PC-FMCW-informed} only in the sense that it reuses the repository's downstream analytical geometry-to-link abstraction. The latent quantities above are not measured optical calibration parameters and the equations are not presented as a complete physical channel model.

\section{Bayesian Calibration and Information Value}
\subsection{Discrete Belief and Causal Update}
C1--C3 maintain a discrete Bayesian belief over a $3\times3\times3$ support. The broad support is
\begin{align}
\alpha_{\mathrm{loss}}&\in\{0.85,1.00,1.15\},\\
\delta_{\mathrm{beam}}&\in\{-0.04,0,0.04\}\ \mathrm{rad},\\
k_{\mathrm{angular}}&\in\{0.75,1.00,1.25\}.
\end{align}
Scenario D uses a narrower support around the nominal model. Unless otherwise specified, support hypotheses begin with equal prior mass.

The communication observation is modeled SNR corrupted by Gaussian noise with $\sigma_z=1$~dB. Crucially, the update occurs \emph{after} the selected first action is executed. If $b_k(\phi_m)$ is the prior mass of hypothesis $m$ and $z_{k+1}$ is the just-realized observation, then
\begin{equation}
b_{k+1}(\phi_m)
\propto
b_k(\phi_m)
\exp\!\left[
-\frac{(z_{k+1}-\mu_z(\phi_m))^2}{2\sigma_z^2}
\right].
\end{equation}
Thus an observation caused by a control action cannot influence selection of that same action.

\subsection{Predictive-Information Proxy}
For a candidate trajectory, the implementation evaluates at most four future measurements. If $\mu_{m,j}$ is the modeled SNR for hypothesis $m$ at future index $j$, the posterior predictive variance is
\begin{equation}
V_j=\sum_m b_k(\phi_m)
\left(\mu_{m,j}-\sum_\ell b_k(\phi_\ell)\mu_{\ell,j}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The information score is
\begin{equation}
G(\tau)
=
\sum_{j=1}^{H_I}
\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(1+\frac{V_j}{\sigma_z^2}\right),
\qquad H_I\le 4.
\end{equation}
This is a Gaussian predictive-information approximation. It is not exact mutual information, does not integrate the future closed-loop posterior tree, and is not a physically calibrated Fisher-information measure.

\section{Decision-Triggered Active Self-Calibration}
\subsection{Decision Diagnostics}
For each safe candidate and each belief hypothesis, the planner forms a cost matrix $J(\tau_i,\phi_m)$. The posterior-expected candidate cost is
\begin{equation}
\bar J(\tau_i)=\sum_m b_k(\phi_m)J(\tau_i,\phi_m),
\end{equation}
and the belief-optimal baseline is
\begin{equation}
\bar\tau=\arg\min_{\tau_i\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{safe}}_k}\bar J(\tau_i).
\end{equation}

Two diagnostics quantify whether model uncertainty matters to the decision:
\begin{align}
U_D
&=
P_{\phi\sim b_k}
\!\left[
\arg\min_{\tau_i}J(\tau_i,\phi)\neq\bar\tau
\right],\\
R_D
&=
E_{\phi\sim b_k}
\!\left[
J(\bar\tau,\phi)-\min_{\tau_i}J(\tau_i,\phi)
\right].
\end{align}
$U_D$ is the posterior mass for which the trajectory ranking disagrees with the belief-optimal candidate; $R_D$ measures the expected cost of committing to that candidate when a different candidate is optimal under a plausible hypothesis.

For C1, candidate selection minimizes $\bar J(\tau)$. When active information seeking is enabled, the implementation also computes a normalized first-control deviation plus a small mobility-excess penalty $P(\tau)$ relative to $\bar\tau$. C2 uses
\begin{equation}
S_{C2}(\tau)=
\bar J(\tau)+\lambda_P P(\tau)-\lambda_I G(\tau).
\end{equation}
C3 first evaluates the gate
\begin{equation}
I_k=
\mathbb{1}
\!\left[
U_D>\eta_D
\ \wedge\
R_D>\eta_R
\right],
\end{equation}
and then scores
\begin{equation}
S_{C3}(\tau)=
\bar J(\tau)+\lambda_P P(\tau)
-
I_k\,\lambda_I U_D G(\tau).
\end{equation}
The selected frozen setting is $\eta_D=0.10$, $\eta_R=0$, $\lambda_I=0.25$, and $\lambda_P=0.20$. A probe is recorded when information seeking is active and the selected candidate differs from the passive belief-optimal baseline.

C4 receives the true simulator $\phi^\star$ only for connectivity scoring. It uses the same target prediction, candidate generator, and hard-safety filters as C0--C3. It therefore provides a model-relative parameter oracle, not privileged future motion truth and not a safety oracle.

\subsection{Simulation and Candidate Settings}
The simulation timestep is 0.1~s and each scenario contains 31 states. The causal predictor uses eight history steps and produces a 16-step planning horizon after a three-observation startup guard. Target-position observation noise has standard deviation 0.20~m. The communication observation noise has standard deviation 1~dB. The information horizon is four steps.

Static and dynamic stop-viability checks, bounded brake/steer dynamics, time-aligned target clearance, and hierarchical clearance handling are enabled for every planner. These implementation choices are inherited from the common planning infrastructure and are held fixed across C0--C4.

\subsection{Development, Freeze, and Confirmation}
Development seeds are 31000--31019. The predeclared grid contains three values each for decision threshold, information weight, and probe weight, giving 27 C2/C3 settings. C0, C1, and C4 are common controls. A setting is eligible only if all reconstructed C0--C4 development episodes satisfy zero collisions, zero no-candidate episodes, and zero static-clearance violations. Among eligible settings, the deterministic rule lexicographically minimizes C3 mean cumulative decision regret, C3 cumulative probe cost, C3 probe fraction, and finally the setting identifier.

The selected setting is \texttt{dev\_t010\_i025\_p020}. The protocol was frozen at 2026-09-25 07:28:10 UTC. Confirmatory seeds 32000--32049 were then opened without further tuning. The confirmatory artifact contains
\[
50\ \text{seeds}\times 6\ \text{scenarios}\times 5\ \text{planners}
=1500
\]
episode rows and 45,000 timestep rows.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
The primary endpoint is cumulative oracle-parameter decision regret. After a candidate is selected, its evaluation-only regret is
\begin{equation}
r_k=
J(\tau_k,\phi^\star)
-
\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{safe}}_k}
J(\tau,\phi^\star),
\end{equation}
and negative numerical roundoff is clipped at zero before accumulation. The oracle computation occurs only after candidate selection and cannot affect C0--C3 actions.

Six scenarios are averaged within seed before inferential testing, so the independent confirmatory unit is the seed ($n=50$). The predeclared comparison families are C0--C1, C1--C2, C2--C3, and C3--C4. Seven metrics are analyzed within each family: cumulative regret, oracle-parameter agreement, probe fraction, cumulative probe cost, final normalized parameter error, progress, and mean outage probability.

For each metric and comparison, the analysis reports a deterministic paired bootstrap 95\% interval with 10,000 resamples, a two-sided paired Wilcoxon test, Cohen's $d_z$, and rank-biserial effect size. Wilcoxon $p$-values are Holm adjusted across the seven metrics within each planner-comparison family. Scenario rows and timesteps are diagnostics and are never treated as independent confirmatory replicates.

\subsection{Learning More Did Not Mean Deciding Better}
The calibration metrics expose the main mechanism. C1 reduced normalized parameter error relative to C0 by 0.393495 (95\% CI $[-0.423885,-0.363191]$, Holm-adjusted $p\approx1.24\times10^{-14}$). C2 reduced parameter error by a further 0.053484 relative to C1 (95\% CI $[-0.079561,-0.026464]$, Holm-adjusted $p\approx1.30\times10^{-4}$), but incurred dramatically larger decision regret.

C3 moved in the opposite direction: relative to C2, its parameter error was 0.046795 higher (95\% CI $[0.020566,0.072209]$, Holm-adjusted $p\approx4.24\times10^{-4}$) while its decision regret was 0.082211 lower. Thus better parameter estimation and better downstream decisions are empirically distinct outcomes in this benchmark.

C3 also reduced probe fraction by 0.037444 relative to C2 (95\% CI $[-0.039778,-0.035333]$, Holm-adjusted $p\approx3.10\times10^{-9}$) and reduced cumulative probe cost by 0.020332 (95\% CI $[-0.025588,-0.015622]$, same adjusted significance order).

\subsection{Safety, Mobility, and Communication}
Across all 1,500 confirmatory episodes, the sampled hard gate recorded zero collision episodes, zero no-candidate steps, and zero static-clearance-violation steps. The minimum recorded static-obstacle clearance was approximately 1.500003~m. This supports the statement that the declared benchmark completed without these failures; it is not a general safety guarantee.

Mean progress was almost unchanged across planners, approximately 24.76~m overall. C2 gained only about 0.0198~m relative to C1 while incurring the much larger regret and probing burden above. Mean outage probability increased by about 0.005105 for C2 relative to C1 and decreased by about 0.006654 for C3 relative to C2; both effects survived Holm correction. C4 should not be interpreted as an outage-minimizing controller because it optimizes the same mixed mobility/connectivity objective using true model parameters.

\subsection{Measured Vehicular Visible-Light Path Loss}
The V-VLC study uses the public measurement data associated with Turan and Coleri's machine-learning channel-modeling work \cite{turan2021}. Distance-only held-out spatial-group MAE is 5.587261~dB and the directional model MAE is 5.580011~dB. The group-paired directional-minus-distance-only absolute-error effect is $-0.005031$~dB with 95\% CI $[-0.019734,0.010134]$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.375269$.

That result is essentially null for a meaningful directional predictive advantage in the measured dataset. It therefore limits, rather than strengthens, physical claims about the synthetic directional latent family. Broader V-VLC literature shows that geometry, weather, reflections, vehicle motion, and receiver field of view can matter materially \cite{karbalayghareh2020,turan2022nlos,mohamed2023,cailean2024}; our measured comparison simply does not show that the particular added directional features improve this held-out regression task.

\subsection{Measured 5G V2X Prediction and Replay}
The CICV5G support study uses the measured dataset of Zhang \emph{et al.} \cite{zhang2026}, comprising 38 W2S drives and 43,045 samples in the analyzed subset. Persistence is the strongest tested one-step delay baseline in the primary split with MAE 5.576288~ms. At longer horizons, route/horizon fusion improves delay prediction relative to persistence across five reused-drive grouped split assignments, and the offline replay produces a descriptive mean P2--P1 measured-delay effect of approximately $-0.501375$~ms.

These results support measured QoS/pose transfer and offline replay methodology. The split assignments reuse the same finite drives and are not independent replications. Most importantly, 5G delay measurements do not calibrate an optical channel and do not validate the PC-FMCW-informed directional latent model.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the Frozen Study Supports}
The strongest result is not that active calibration beats passive calibration. It is that \emph{unconditional} information seeking can be highly damaging, and that downstream decision relevance is an effective guard against much of that damage. C2 learns the modeled parameter vector more aggressively, but pays for that information with higher regret, higher probing burden, and worse mean outage. C3 keeps most of the benefits of passive adaptation while largely refusing probes that do not alter the motion decision.

Scenario E makes this point especially cleanly. The posterior remains uncertain, yet C3 does nothing because that uncertainty does not change the preferred safe trajectory. This is precisely the distinction between parameter uncertainty and decision-relevant uncertainty.

\subsection{Why Decision Relevance Was Not Sufficient}
Scenario F provides the complementary boundary. C3 correctly identifies decision disagreement and does probe, but the probe does not improve regret relative to passive C1. Several explanations are plausible: the candidate lattice may not contain a sufficiently informative low-cost experiment; the four-step information horizon may be too short; the predictive-variance proxy may value measurements that do not resolve the particular ranking ambiguity; or the relative scaling between task cost, probe cost, and information reward may be imperfect. The frozen data do not identify which explanation dominates, so these remain hypotheses for future work rather than retrospective claims.

This negative result is consistent with the broader dual-control literature: creating a useful dual effect requires more than simply adding an exploration bonus \cite{mesbah2018,hu2024}. The quality of the belief model, future-information approximation, action set, and safety mechanism determines whether exploration has positive decision value.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Calibration and Communication-Aware Planning}
Informative path planning and calibration trajectory design often evaluate information quality or parameter observability directly \cite{hitz2017,lv2022,amersdorfer2025}. Our evaluation changes the endpoint: information is useful only insofar as it reduces downstream trajectory-selection loss. This does not replace observability analysis; rather, it highlights a decision layer above it.

Likewise, communication-aware and ISAC-aware planners already adapt motion to link quality or sensing utility \cite{ghaffarkhah2011,gordon2026,jin2026}. Here the communication objective itself depends on an uncertain model, and motion can alter the future belief about that model. That additional coupling motivates the C1/C2/C3 decomposition.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\subsection{Model Validity}
The optical-link equations, latent parameters, and Gaussian SNR observation noise are modeled. No measured PC-FMCW calibration is claimed. The V-VLC support study is not used to fit the three latent parameters, and its null directional comparison argues against treating the simulator mechanism as experimentally validated.

\subsection{Identifiability and Action Richness}
Identifiability is conditional on the geometry exposed by the candidate lattice. A different set of safe maneuvers, a longer information horizon, or a richer latent family could change both posterior learning and decision disagreement. The distance-only ablation intentionally makes two angular latents non-identifiable and demonstrates that the planner does not force artificial convergence.

\subsection{Approximate Information Value}
$G(\tau)$ sums marginal predictive-variance terms under the current belief. It does not solve the exact Bayesian dual-control problem, propagate all possible future observations, or account for future replanning inside the information objective. Accordingly, C3 is best described as a decision-triggered active-calibration heuristic grounded in dual-control principles, not an optimal dual controller.

\subsection{Statistical Generalization}
The confirmatory unit is the independent simulator seed within six fixed mechanism scenarios. The absence of sampled safety failures is not a safety guarantee, and statistical conclusions apply only to the declared benchmark distribution. The C1--C3 comparison was not predeclared as a primary inferential family and is kept descriptive.

\subsection{Measured-Support Scope}
The V-VLC and CICV5G studies answer different empirical questions. The former tests measured vehicular optical path-loss prediction; the latter tests 5G QoS prediction and offline replay. Neither is a closed-loop real-vehicle validation of active optical self-calibration.

\section{Conclusion}
We evaluated whether active calibration should be triggered by uncertainty in a modeled directional vehicular link or by the \emph{decision consequences} of that uncertainty. Under the frozen controlled benchmark, unconditional information seeking substantially worsened decision regret despite improving parameter estimation. Decision-triggered C3 removed most of that penalty, sharply reduced probing, and correctly remained inactive when uncertainty was decision irrelevant.

The result has an equally important negative boundary. C3 did not demonstrate a confirmatory advantage over passive C1, and in the decision-critical scenario F it probed without lowering regret. A distance-only development ablation eliminated the active mechanism, while measured V-VLC data provided essentially null support for directional predictive gain.

The supported conclusion is therefore deliberately narrow: \emph{decision relevance is useful for preventing unnecessary active calibration, but the implemented probing policy does not yet demonstrate a confirmatory advantage over passive Bayesian calibration}. Future work should focus on richer safe probe actions and more faithful approximations of downstream value of information rather than simply increasing information gain.
